The several orders of a story

On this page
  1. The bell rang once
  2. A clue is a relationship, not only an object
  3. Belief is not the whole state
  4. When a reading changes the invention
  5. Following a change through the story

A story has more than one order. There is the order in which things happen, the order in which its characters understand them, and the order in which the reader finds out. A mystery depends on their difference: an event can be over before anyone understands it, and a reader can suspect something that a character has yet to see. That gap is something I can write with.

The bell rang once

Imagine a bell ringing at noon. One witness hears it, another sees a stopped clock, and a third arrives later. The narrative shows the clock first, introduces the bell later, and only then lets us hear the first witness's account. Moving that account earlier would change the reader's experience without changing when the bell rang; changing a witness's belief might change their next action while leaving the bell untouched. “Move this earlier” might mean moving the event, a memory of it, or its appearance on the page. Each sends me back to different scenes.

Suppose the first witness leaves at 12:15. Moving the bell from noon to 13:00 would remove their chance to hear it. Showing their account earlier changes what the reader knows while leaving those event times intact. The Notes follow the scenes each revision would affect and the choices it leaves me.

The clock's face is an observation, not a reliable timestamp for the bell. Its place in the reader's sequence can suggest a connection before the story gives enough information to judge it.

A clue is a relationship, not only an object

The stopped clock might establish a location, suggest a time, or mislead someone who assumes it is running. If I change where it hangs, the witness's opportunity to see it may disappear. If I change when the reader sees it, the opportunity remains but the inference changes. The clue lives in these relations, not only in the inventory of objects. A later scene may depend on the clock having been seen, on a mistaken conclusion about it, or on the reader remembering its face.

Information architecture helps when it makes those dependencies legible. Instead of rewriting every scene that mentions a clock, I can follow the opportunity, belief and action that the revised premise affected. Where a dependency is uncertain, the uncertainty is itself useful: it exposes a creative choice that still needs an answer.

Belief is not the whole state

Continue the bell example. Suppose the late arrival is frightened when the two witnesses meet later, and the first witness offers support without explaining the event. Trust between them might improve while neither person's knowledge of the bell's cause changes. If a model of the story folds trust and confidence about causes into one score, support becomes evidence by accident. Keeping those states distinct allows a person to feel safer with somebody while still doubting their account.

An institution introduces another path. It receives a genuine account of the bell, but without the stopped clock that would have made it doubt the time. Its staff can become more confident and choose the wrong intervention without anybody inventing a false observation. Restoring the clock to the account changes that inference and the decisions based on it. It need not erase the trust that grew between the witnesses, or undo the consequences of the intervention already made. State changes travel through their particular relationships; one correction is not a reset of the world.

The drawings can change too. A remembered scene might lose colour or break into fragments as a witness begins to doubt their interpretation. The bell still rang at noon. Fear might change how the witness is framed without moving the clock. The image needs to let us distinguish whose experience changed and what they are remembering; otherwise a changed palette can look like a changed event.

In a model that keeps them apart, actions leave something behind in people, relationships and institutions. Accounts can be corrected without erasing consequences, and the same occurrence can be encountered in several forms. The rules are choices about a story, not measurements of human psychology. They are useful if they show me a consequence I would otherwise miss.

When a reading changes the invention

A traditional passage can change what I want an invented rule to do. Following a symbol through its transformations may make its fictional role seem too static, or reveal a consequence I had missed. I might revise that role or keep it as an independent invention. The relation is useful because the reading can push back on the work: an earlier image need not dictate what the passage means, and a passage need not dictate the only story I can make.

Following a change through the story

The story model I am developing for Sleepwalker keeps track of its people and objects, their changing states, the order of scenes, transformations and the images through which we encounter them. An altered observation might unsettle a character's next choice, while an illustration might expose a relation that the prose left vague. Following those changes can send the work in a new direction.

I still have to decide what to do with what the model shows me. Eventually the reader encounters none of this machinery, only the space between panels, a remembered object, an account that arrives late. The several orders become worthwhile when their arrangement lets that reader discover the relation for themselves.

Notes

Following a revision through the bell example

The essay uses a neutral mystery example, not a Sleepwalker scene. To examine a revision, give that example a few additional stipulated facts: the first witness leaves at 12:15, the third arrives at 12:30, and the bell rings once at noon. The first witness hears it; the third learns about it through the later account. The stopped clock is a separate observation, not a reliable timestamp for the ringing.

Moving the event to one o'clock

This edit invalidates the first witness's opportunity to hear the bell under the stated schedule. It also makes direct hearing possible for the third witness, assuming they remain there. The narrative can retain the first witness's account only by making a further choice: change the departure, change the account's source, or make the account mistaken. Each choice leads to a different story.

The revision therefore travels through event → presence → opportunity → knowledge → any action depending on that knowledge. It does not automatically travel through every reference to the clock. That object becomes affected only if a scene has connected it to the changed event.

Moving the account earlier in the narrative

Keep all event times unchanged and show the first witness's account before the stopped clock. Each witness still has the same opportunity to hear or see what happened. The reader, however, now approaches the clock with knowledge of the bell. An apparent timestamp may look suspicious sooner. This revision travels through reveal → reader inference → later surprise or recognition, without changing the world chronology.

Changing a belief

Suppose the second witness interprets the stopped clock as the time of the bell. Correcting that belief need not change the observed face of the clock. It changes whatever inference or action used the reading as a timestamp. A scene that only describes the clock can remain intact; a scene that relies on the mistaken time needs reconsideration.

An ambiguous editorial instruction has become several concrete choices with different consequences. A graph of these stipulated relationships would expose the affected scenes; deciding which revision makes the better story remains the author's work.

Revision Dependency that changes What can remain intact
Ring at 13:00 instead of noon Presence → hearing opportunity → knowledge A clock description with no asserted link to the bell
Show the account before the clock Reader knowledge → inference → later reveal World chronology and witness presence
Correct the second witness's timestamp belief Interpretation → action based on that time The observed clock face and the noon event

Following the particular relation, rather than every mention of the object, keeps a small revision from becoming an indiscriminate rewrite.
