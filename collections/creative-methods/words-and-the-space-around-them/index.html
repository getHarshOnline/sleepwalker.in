Words and the space around them

Creative methods
  • Words and the space around them
  • From frame to sequence
Collection overview
On this page

Put “I saw it” beside two figures. A balloon pointing to the nearer figure makes one person the witness; a tail reaching towards the other changes who claims to have seen. Put the words in an established memory caption instead, and the moment of speaking may lie elsewhere. The sentence has not changed. Its place on the page has changed the account it gives us.

That makes lettering part of the story's evidence. If a later panel reveals that the apparent speaker was repeating somebody else's account, the first arrangement must have left room for that discovery. An arbitrary tail or a decorative change of type would spend the reader's attention on a relation the story never meant to offer. Ambiguity can be deliberate, but it needs a relationship worth being uncertain about.

A voice reaches the page

A line of speech can reach across a boundary before its speaker is visible. The reader encounters a voice, then discovers where it came from. Detached text can suggest another register only if its typography and position give a reason to read it differently. I want that difference to survive beyond colour: a person's speech, a machine translation and a remembered voice should remain distinguishable through shape, spacing and consistent conventions.

The convention earns trust through ordinary use. Once a reader knows how a remembered voice looks, a departure can become consequential. If every line arrives in a newly invented treatment, the departure has no baseline. The page asks the reader to learn a new code each time instead of following what the voice says.

Lettering also makes a route through a panel. Two balloons can be legible separately and still leave their order uncertain. Moving a line to make room for an image can change who seems to answer whom. I would review the exchange at the size it will actually be read, including a phone: the words, their order and their relation to the figures need to hold together. Enlarging a beautiful frame does not repair an exchange that cannot be followed.

A mark can extend beyond its pieces

Negative space can hold an unfinished relation rather than merely make a page elegant. Consider fragments of an invented glyph placed in successive panels. Their alignment could leave a shape in the space between them that no individual panel contains. Moving the fragments would alter that shape without changing the fragments themselves. This is one way an invented script could carry meaning in an arrangement, not only in its separate marks.

The gap needs to do something visible. If the pieces form the same complete mark inside each panel, their repetition only decorates the sequence. If one fragment becomes readable through its relation to another, moving or closing the interval changes the reading. A narrow screen might also separate the fragments and remove that relation. The composition would then need another treatment, not merely smaller lettering.

The action left between panels asks a reader to supply what is missing. Words and marks can use that space differently: locating a speaker, carrying a memory or completing an invented sign. Empty space becomes articulate when changing it changes what can be read.
