# What the witness asks for

What would I ask for after seeing a world inside a child? Greater sight, perhaps, or an explanation of how the familiar hermitage could exist in that impossible setting. Markandeya's next encounter takes the question elsewhere. The witness has seen something immense; what he asks to keep is a relationship.

<a id="what-follows-the-extraordinary-encounter"></a>

## What follows the extraordinary encounter?

The familiar image of Markandeya inside a child's breath can end with cosmic amazement. The *Bhāgavata* continues. After the encounter, [12.10.2](https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/12/10/2/) gives the sage a prayer in which māyā can appear in the guise of knowledge and bewilder even divine beings. In the Bhaktivedanta Book Trust translation, he responds by taking shelter in Hari. The narrative's answer is devotional, not a better diagram of the universe. That changes the question I bring back to the encounter: what does seeing something immense do to the witness's confidence in what he knows?

A spectacular revelation often gives a fictional character new explanatory power. Here I find another possibility. The experience can alter the relation to explanation itself. If apparent knowledge can participate in bewilderment, another impressive account need not resolve the difficulty. That is my reading of the narrative turn, rather than an argument that studying the world is futile. The text's devotional response and my question about confidence meet without becoming the same statement.

<a id="the-heart-is-another-setting"></a>

## The heart is another setting

The next encounter moves inward. In [12.10.9](https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/12/10/9/), Markandeya's absorption is so complete that he does not notice Shiva and Uma arriving. [12.10.10](https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/12/10/10/) describes Shiva entering the hidden space of his heart, comparing the entrance to air passing through an opening. The cosmographic witness is now the site of an encounter he did not perceive externally. The narrative can make an enormous world intimate without shrinking its significance.

What attracts me is the movement between settings. The sage recognises his hermitage inside the cosmic child; later a divine visitor enters the sage's interior. A simple inside-and-outside diagram cannot carry the whole relation, because whose interior it is and how the encounter happens both change. The text makes the reader follow a person through those changes. That attention to encounter is richer than assigning each figure one permanent function.

When a boon is offered, [12.10.34](https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/12/10/34/) has Markandeya ask for unwavering devotion to the Lord, the Lord's devotees and Shiva. The BBT commentary explicitly interprets Shiva's place through its Vaishnava theology. That interpretive setting matters. It is not a neutral legend in which familiar names can be freely substituted, nor a statement that every tradition arranges their relationships this way.

The sequence has moved twice: from the witness seeing an immense world to a visitor entering the witness, then from extraordinary perception to a request about devotion. The boon does not merely reward how much he has seen. It concerns the relation he wishes to keep. That makes the final request an essential part of my reading, rather than an epilogue after the interesting cosmic scene.

<a id="recognition-does-not-make-every-account-one-biography"></a>

## Recognition does not make every account one biography

The short-life Markandeya, the encounter with death and Shiva, and the witness to dissolution invite comparison. They should not be joined merely because the name is familiar. Even within these two adjacent *Bhāgavata* chapters, a close reading discovers different relations: witness and world, knowledge and bewilderment, inner absorption and external arrival, gift and devotion. A comparison with another text needs the particular account it contributes, not a generic “Markandeya story” assembled from memory.

This is where the inquiry becomes personally useful. I begin with a name I recognise, follow a passage until it changes the question, and return to the name with less certainty but more substance. The [world inside the child](https://sleepwalker.in/collections/vedic-world-inquiry/through-markandeyas-encounters/markandeya-and-the-size-of-a-world/) remains immense, but its witness no longer appears only as someone who saw it. His absorption and the devotion he requests give the encounter a life beyond its spectacle. Sometimes that reading changes the fiction. Sometimes it changes only the reader. Both are reasons to continue.
